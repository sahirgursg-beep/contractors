How to find and bid on federal construction contracts

SAM.gov registration, NAICS codes and size standards, set-asides, Miller Act bonds and Davis-Bacon wages: what a contractor needs before bidding on federal construction.

Federal agencies buy billions of dollars of construction every year, from roof repairs at a post office to new buildings at military bases. Most of it is advertised publicly on SAM.gov, and small businesses get a large share through set-asides. The paperwork is front-loaded: get it done once and every bid after that is easier.

1. Register in SAM.gov

To be awarded a federal contract, your business must be registered in the System for Award Management (SAM.gov). Registration gives you a Unique Entity ID (UEI) and asks for your legal business details, banking information, NAICS codes and representations and certifications. Registration is free and must be renewed every year; be wary of companies that charge to “register” you.

2. Choose your NAICS codes and check your size

Construction work falls under NAICS sector 23: 236 for building construction, 237 for heavy and civil engineering, and 238 for specialty trades. Each code has a small business size standard set by the SBA, usually measured by average annual receipts. If your receipts are under the standard for the code on a solicitation, you count as small for that contract.

3. Understand set-asides

Many solicitations are reserved for small businesses or for programs such as 8(a), HUBZone, service-disabled veteran-owned (SDVOSB) and women-owned (WOSB). The solicitation states the set-aside; you need the matching status or certification before you bid.

4. Bonds

Under the Miller Act, federal construction contracts over $150,000 require performance and payment bonds, normally each for 100% of the contract price, and a bid guarantee (bid bond) when bonds are required. For contracts over $35,000 up to $150,000 the contracting officer requires alternative payment protection, such as a payment bond or an irrevocable letter of credit. Talk to a surety early: your bonding capacity limits the size of the jobs you can win.

5. Davis-Bacon wages

Federal construction contracts over $2,000 are covered by the Davis-Bacon Act: you must pay laborers and mechanics at least the prevailing wages and fringe benefits in the wage determination attached to the solicitation, and submit certified payrolls weekly. Price your bid with those rates, not your usual pay scale.

6. Read the notice type

  • Sources sought / RFI: market research. Responding is how you tell the agency small businesses can do the work, which can lead to a set-aside.
  • Presolicitation: advance notice that a solicitation is coming.
  • Solicitation: the actual invitation for bids (IFB) or request for proposals (RFP), with a response deadline.

7. Still need a state license

A federal contract does not replace state and local licensing. Many states require a contractor license for work on federal property in the state, and subcontractors in licensed trades still need their licenses.
